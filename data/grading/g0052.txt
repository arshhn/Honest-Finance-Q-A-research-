Gold answer: Yes. Multiple lawsuits have been filed against Boeing resulting from a 2018 Lion Air crash and a 2019 Ethiopian Airlines crash.

Candidate answer: Yes. Boeing's FY2022 Form 10-K discloses several material ongoing legal matters, nearly all tied to the 737 MAX. The principal ones are: (1) civil litigation from the Lion Air Flight 610 (Oct 2018) and Ethiopian Airlines Flight 302 (Mar 2019) crashes — wrongful-death/personal-injury suits by victims' families, most filed in the U.S. District Court for the Northern District of Illinois, many of which Boeing has settled or is settling but which remain a source of loss exposure; (2) the January 2021 Deferred Prosecution Agreement with the U.S. Department of Justice resolving a criminal conspiracy-to-defraud-the-FAA charge for roughly $2.5 billion total (a $243.6 million criminal penalty, ~$1.77 billion in airline customer compensation, and a $500 million crash-victim beneficiaries fund), under which Boeing remained subject to a three-year DPA term and continuing obligations (the DPA's term ran into January 2024, and victims' families were litigating in the Northern District of Texas seeking to reopen it under the Crime Victims' Rights Act); (3) a federal securities class action in the Northern District of Illinois brought on behalf of purchasers of Boeing stock alleging misstatements about 737 MAX safety and the MCAS system; (4) shareholder derivative actions against current and former directors and officers alleging breach of fiduciary duty in board oversight of MAX safety — the Delaware Court of Chancery derivative litigation was settled in FY2022 for approximately $237.5 million, funded essentially entirely by D&O insurance; and (5) various other matters including commercial disputes/claims from airline customers over 737 MAX and 787 delivery delays, an Embraer arbitration arising from the terminated strategic partnership, and government investigations/subpoenas (SEC, DOJ, Congress) relating to the MAX and to 787 production and quality issues. Boeing states in the 10-K that it cannot reasonably estimate a range of loss for certain of these matters and that outcomes could be material to its results of operations, financial position, or cash flows.
